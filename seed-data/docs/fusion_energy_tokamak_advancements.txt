# Magnetic Confinement Fusion: Tokamak Dynamics and High-Temperature Superconductors

Magnetic confinement fusion aims to recreate the energetic reactions of stellar cores by heating deuterium-tritium (D-T) fuel mixtures into high-temperature plasmas exceeding 100 million Kelvin (approximately 10 to 15 keV). At these extreme temperatures, the Coulomb barrier between atomic nuclei is overcome, generating energetic helium-4 alpha particles (3.5 MeV) and high-energy neutrons (14.1 MeV).

The Lawson criterion defines the threshold for net energy gain ($Q > 1$), requiring the triple product of plasma density ($n$), confinement time ($\tau_E$), and ion temperature ($T$) to exceed $3 \times 10^{21} \text{ m}^{-3} \text{s keV}$.

Tokamaks employ helical magnetic fields created by toroidal field coils and an induced poloidal field driven by a central solenoid. The recent deployment of Rare-Earth Barium Copper Oxide (REBCO) High-Temperature Superconductor (HTS) tape allows magnetic field strengths exceeding 20 Tesla at the magnetic axis. Because fusion power density scales proportionally to the fourth power of the magnetic field ($B^4$), HTS magnets enable compact tokamak designs with dramatically reduced volume and capital expense compared to ITER.

Key plasma instabilities, including Edge Localized Modes (ELMs) and neoclassical tearing modes, are actively suppressed via resonant magnetic perturbations (RMP) and continuous electron cyclotron resonance heating (ECRH).
